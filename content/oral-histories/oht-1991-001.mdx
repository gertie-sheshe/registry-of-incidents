I have attended the St. Cyprian's Church Annual Harvest Fundraiser every year since I was nineteen years old. In 1987 I would have been thirty-four. I was sitting in Pew 3, on the left side of the church, which is where I have always sat. My mother sat there before me. I was not near the serving table. I was near enough to see it clearly, and I have always had good eyes, and on that afternoon there was nothing in my line of sight between my pew and the eastern wall where the table stood.

I remember that the serving table was set in its usual place beneath the second window. Mama Edna's sufuria was at the centre, as it always was. I had been thinking about the pilau since the morning. I had not eaten breakfast, which was a habit I had on the days of the fundraiser so that I would be hungry enough to do the pilau proper justice. Father Kamau had been speaking for some time about the prizes. Gerald Otieno was at the front table, as he often was — positioned close to the serving area, in the way that certain men arrange themselves at these occasions so as to be first served.

I saw the sufuria move. I cannot say with certainty whether I saw it from the very beginning of its movement or only from the moment before it struck the stand. The sound it made when it hit was not loud. It was a particular kind of sound — a sound that does not belong inside a church, a sound of metal and cheap wood and wire, and because it did not belong there it was heard by every person in the room simultaneously. The stand came down. The tickets went into the air and across the floor. Father Kamau stopped speaking. Every head turned. The whole room was still for a moment in a way I have not experienced in any church since.

I have replayed that moment every day for thirty-seven years. The sound the sufuria made. The silence that followed. And then Gerald. Gerald Otieno stood up. I do not know why he stood up. He has said, when asked, that he stood because he was close to the stand and felt he ought to help. But the way he stood — it was not the way a person stands when they mean to help. I have thought about this for thirty-seven years and I cannot explain it further than that. I have not spoken to Gerald Otieno directly about what happened since 1992, when we disagreed about the table.

The raffle was not drawn that afternoon. The grand prize was never given. I held ticket number 47. I do not know whether ticket number 47 was among the ones eventually recovered from the floor. I still have the half of the ticket that was given to me when I paid my contribution. I have kept it in a biscuit tin for thirty-seven years. When the Heritage Trust archivist came to see me in 1991, I showed it to her. She photographed it. She asked if I wanted it back when they were finished with it. I told her to keep it. I do not need it to remember.
